🎯 How to Answer Every Question Type

This section translates the marking guidelines into a repeatable method. The golden rules come straight from the memo's own instructions (present in every year's marking guideline):

The memo's marking rules (verbatim principles, 2021–2025)

  • If you give two answers and the first is wrong, the first one is marked — don't hedge with two guesses.
  • A spelling error only costs a mark if it changes the meaning.
  • You are not penalised for omitting inverted commas when quoting.
  • Open-ended questions: “no marks for YES/NO or I AGREE/DISAGREE — the reason/substantiation is what is considered.”
  • TRUE/FALSE: same rule — the reason earns the mark, not the word TRUE/FALSE.
  • Number answers correctly, and start each section on a new page.

The command-word ladder (what the verb demands)

Identify / Name1The correct term (device, tone, character)One word
Describe2Two factual elements (e.g. time and place)One sentence, two parts
Explain2A point + its reason (or two linked points)1–2 sentences

How to answer the 1-mark questions

What the examiner wants: pure recall/recognition. The memo lists a single correct answer (or a small set of acceptable synonyms).

Approach: answer only what is asked — a letter (A–D), one word, one fact. No sentences.

Earns full marks: the exact term/fact, spelled acceptably (a spelling that doesn't change meaning is still credited).

Common mistakes: writing a paragraph for a 1-mark question (wastes time); giving two answers (the first is marked); misreading “FALSE” as “TRUE”.

❌ WeakQ: Identify the figure of speech in line 4.
“I think it might be a metaphor because it compares two things, and also maybe personification since…”
✅ Full marksQ: Identify the figure of speech in line 4.
“Metaphor”

How to answer “Explain” (2 marks)

What the examiner wants: the memo almost always pairs an answer with a reason, often ending “NOTE: Both parts must be included to earn the marks.”

Approach: state the point, then attach “because / as / since…” with the reason — or give two distinct facts.

Earns full marks: both halves present. Example from the 2025 memo: “Macbeth is anxious/nervous as he agonises about his footsteps being heard.” (emotion + reason).

Common mistakes: giving only the emotion or only the reason; explaining the wrong line; restating the quote without explaining it.

❌ Weak (1/2)Q: Explain the irony in Macbeth's words, “And purge it to a sound and pristine health.”
“Macbeth wants Scotland to be healthy.”
✅ Full marks (2/2)Same question
“Macbeth pleads with the Doctor to heal Scotland, yet he is the very cause of its suffering.”

How to answer “Identify the figure of speech… Explain why it is relevant” (1 + 2)

What the examiner wants: (a) the correct device name — 1 mark; (b) an explanation using the memo's “in the same way that… so too…” comparison structure.

Approach: (a) name it. (b) break the image into its two halves and link them: “Just as X [literally]…, so too Y [the figurative meaning].”

Earns full marks: the memo's model answer (2025, Sonnet 73): “Just as the branches of the trees become bare and weak as the seasons change, so too does the speaker's body become fragile as he ages.”

Common mistakes: naming the device but not explaining it (2 marks lost); explaining the device's general meaning instead of its meaning in this line; confusing simile/metaphor/personification.

❌ Weak“It is a metaphor. Metaphors compare things.”
✅ Full marks“Metaphor (1). In the same way that an empty cathedral has no singers, so too the bare branches have no birds — both are deserted and lifeless (2).”

How to answer “Explain the irony” (2 marks)

What the examiner wants: the gap between what is said/appears and what is actually true. The memo states the surface meaning, then reveals the contradiction.

Approach: name the expectation vs the reality. “He says X, yet actually Y.” (Use “yet/however/but” — it forces the contrast the marker is looking for.)

Earns full marks: both sides of the contradiction in one sentence.

Common mistakes: defining irony (“irony is when…”) instead of explaining this irony; giving only the surface meaning; treating sarcasm/coincidence as irony.

❌ Weak“It's ironic because he's lying.”
✅ Full marks“Lady Macbeth welcomes Duncan warmly and says everything will be repaid, yet she is already plotting to have him murdered that very night.”

How to answer “Describe the time and place / setting” (2 marks)

What the examiner wants: two elements — time and place — each worth 1 mark. The memo literally writes “Time: … Place: … NOTE: accept any ONE reference to time and ONE to place.”

Approach: answer with two labelled parts: “Time: … Place: …”. Never give two places and no time.

Earns full marks: a precise time and a precise place, drawn from the extract/poem.

Common mistakes: giving only the place; vague answers (“at night, somewhere in the castle”); describing the mood instead of the setting.

❌ Weak (1/2)“In Macbeth's castle.”
✅ Full marks (2/2)“Time: late at night, before Duncan is murdered. Place: the courtyard of Macbeth's castle (Inverness).”

How to answer “What tone… and why?” (1 + 1)

What the examiner wants: (a) a precise emotion word (the memo accepts a small set, e.g. “Confidence/assurance”); (b) the reason the speaker feels that way.

Approach: (a) one accurate tone word (angry, anxious, sarcastic, confident, pleading…). (b) “because he/she…” with the trigger from the text.

Earns full marks: tone word + reason. Memo example (2025): “(a) Confidence/assurance. (b) He is convinced that Scotland will be returned to its former glory.”

Common mistakes: giving a vague “happy/sad”; giving the tone but no reason; describing what is said instead of how it is said.

❌ Weak“He is sad.”
✅ Full marks“(a) Resigned/despairing. (b) Macbeth has just heard of his wife's death and realises life has lost its meaning.”

How to answer “What does this reveal about [X]'s character? Substantiate” (2 marks)

What the examiner wants: a character trait + proof from the text. The memo pairs the trait with the evidence (“He is principled when he claims that not even money will persuade him to return”).

Approach: trait → because → evidence. Use the word “because” or “when he/she…” to attach the proof.

Earns full marks: trait + substantiation in one sentence. “Substantiate” = quote or point to the exact line/event.

Common mistakes: giving a trait with no proof; quoting without naming the trait; giving a plot summary instead of a character judgement.

❌ Weak (1/2)“The Doctor is a good man.”
✅ Full marks (2/2)“The Doctor is principled/ethical because he says not even money would make him stay in Macbeth's castle.”

How to answer “If you were the director… State TWO actions” (2 marks)

What the examiner wants: two physical, stageable actions (body language, gesture, movement) — not a tone or an opinion. The memo says “Accept any TWO… or any other relevant actions.”

Approach: write two imperative sentences describing what the actor does with their body/voice/position.

Earns full marks: two concrete actions. Memo example (2025): “Siward should move his hands from left to right. He should simulate a circle.”

Common mistakes: giving one action and repeating it; giving a feeling (“he should be angry”) instead of an action; writing stage directions that can't be acted.

❌ Weak“He should be scared and angry.”
✅ Full marks“Macbeth should beat his chest. He should point menacingly at the empty chair.”

How to answer “One of the themes… Discuss this theme” (3 marks)

What the examiner wants: an organised discussion of the theme using 2–3 concrete examples from the text. The memo lists several “viewpoints, among others” and accepts any well-supported angle.

Approach: state the theme in your first sentence, then give 2–3 examples, each tied to a quote/event.

Earns full marks: the memo's own note: “For full marks, the response must be well-substantiated… a candidate can score 1 or 2 marks for a response which is not well-substantiated. The interpretation must be grounded in the play/poem.”

Common mistakes: listing examples with no link to the theme; making a general claim with no text reference; writing one long example instead of two or three.

❌ Weak (1/3)“Ambition is a big theme in Macbeth. Macbeth is very ambitious.”
✅ Full marks (3/3)“Ambition drives the play. Macbeth's ambition is sparked by the witches' prophecy that he will be king, and he admits it ‘o'erleaps' itself when he murders Duncan to seize the crown. Lady Macbeth's ambition is even sharper — she calls on spirits to ‘unsex' her so she can be ruthless. Their shared ambition destroys them both.”

How to answer “Discuss your view” (3 marks, open-ended)

What the examiner wants: a reasoned position (yes or no), supported by 2–3 points from the whole text. The memo gives both a “Yes” list and a “No” list and accepts either — or a combination.

Approach: take a clear stand, then support it with 2–3 points. You may also acknowledge the other side (“However…”).

Earns full marks: memo note: “Do NOT award a mark for YES or NO. Credit responses where a combination is given. For full marks, well-substantiated. 1 or 2 marks if not. Must be grounded in the text.”

Common mistakes: writing “Yes” with no reason; giving your personal opinion with no text reference; changing your position halfway; forgetting the “as a whole” instruction and staying inside the extract only.

❌ Weak (0–1/3)“Yes, I agree that Duncan is a good leader. He is nice.”
✅ Full marks (3/3)“Yes, Duncan is a good leader. He is decisive — he strips the traitor Cawdor of his title and gives it to Macbeth. He recognises bravery and rewards Macbeth and Banquo for their victory over Norway. However, his trust is his flaw, since he misjudges Macbeth and is murdered in his sleep.”

📖 Poetry — Complete Literary Theory

Every device below follows the same five-part pattern: definition → simple example → effect → how it's examined → how to discuss it for marks. The “how it's examined” notes are drawn from real FAL questions and memos.

⚡ The number-one poetry skill in FAL FAL poetry questions are line-anchored — nearly every question says “Refer to line X.” Your job is always the same: name the device/meaning, then explain what it does in that line. Learning a device without learning how to explain its effect earns you the 1 mark but loses the 2.

Figurative language (imagery & comparison)

Metaphor

Definition: a direct comparison that says one thing is another (no “like” or “as”).

Example: “That time of year thou mayst in me behold” — the speaker is autumn/winter.

Effect: creates a vivid, layered image; links the abstract to the concrete; conveys complex emotion economically.

How examined: “Identify the figure of speech (1) + explain its relevance (2).” The memo expects “In the same way that X…, so too Y…”

Discuss it: “The speaker is a metaphor for [X]: just as [literal image], so too [deeper meaning]. This shows…”

Simile

Definition: an explicit comparison using “like” or “as”.

Example: “his rollrock highroad roaring down” is not a simile — a true FAL example: “words hide like …” (Hard to find).

Effect: makes the image clear and accessible; emphasises a particular quality of the thing described.

How examined: same identify + explain format; the memo credits “Simile” as the 1-mark answer.

Discuss it: “The simile ‘X is like Y' emphasises [quality], because Y suggests [connotation].”

Personification

Definition: giving human qualities, feelings or actions to a non-human thing or idea.

Example: “It rounds and rounds Despair to drowning” (Inversnaid) — despair becomes a person who can drown.

Effect: makes the abstract tangible; creates mood; can make nature/ideas feel alive and threatening or gentle.

How examined: extremely common — the memo frequently accepts “Metaphor/Personification” together as valid answers.

Discuss it: “X is personified as [human action], which makes it feel [adjective], creating a [mood] atmosphere.”

Imagery

Definition: language that appeals to the senses (sight, sound, touch, taste, smell) to build a mental picture.

Example: “yellow leaves, or none, or few, do hang” — visual imagery of a bare autumn tree.

Effect: makes the scene concrete and emotional; sets mood; often the vehicle for a deeper theme.

How examined: usually as part of “describe the atmosphere/setting” or theme questions — you explain what the images convey.

Discuss it: “The image of [X] appeals to the sense of [sight/hearing…] and creates a [adjective] mood, suggesting [theme].”

Symbolism

Definition: an object, person or event that stands for a larger idea beyond itself.

Example: in Sonnet 73, the “twilight”/“black night” symbolise approaching death.

Effect: layers meaning; lets the poet discuss big ideas (death, love, freedom) through small concrete things.

How examined: “To what does X refer?” / “What does X symbolise?” — you name the bigger idea it stands for.

Discuss it: “X is a symbol of [idea], because [link], so it reinforces the theme of [theme].”

Irony

Definition: a gap between appearance and reality, or between what is said and what is meant/true.

Example: “Long live the weeds and the wilderness yet” — blessing “weeds” (usually unwanted) as precious.

Effect: creates surprise, criticism, or emotional depth; reveals a truth the surface hides.

How examined: “Explain the irony in…” — the single most reliable 2-mark question in FAL poetry and Macbeth.

Discuss it: “It is ironic that [surface expectation], because in reality [opposite].”

Sound devices

Alliteration

Definition: repetition of the same consonant sound at the start of nearby words.

Example: “rollrock highroad roaring” (Inversnaid).

Effect: draws attention to the words; can echo the sound described (e.g. water's rush) and build rhythm/mood.

How examined: the 2025 paper's “Replace the underlined word” question swapped assonance for alliteration — you must know which is which.

Discuss it: “The alliteration of the [sound] in ‘…' mimics [the thing described] and emphasises [effect].”

Assonance

Definition: repetition of a vowel sound within words (not necessarily at the start).

Example: “flutes and low to the lake falls home” — the long “o” sounds.

Effect: creates internal music; slows or smooths the line; reinforces mood.

How examined: tested as a distractor vs alliteration — know that alliteration = consonants, assonance = vowels.

Discuss it: “The repeated [vowel] sound (‘…') slows the line and creates a [adjective] effect.”

Onomatopoeia

Definition: a word whose sound imitates its meaning.

Example: “roaring”, “shriek” — the 2023 memo credited “onomatopoeia” in Innisfree.

Effect: makes the scene audible and immediate; energises the description.

How examined: often an acceptable answer in “identify the sound device” questions.

Discuss it: “The word ‘X' is onomatopoeic — it reproduces the sound of [thing], making the image vivid.”

Structure, form & voice

Rhyme & rhyme scheme

Definition: matching end-sounds between lines; the pattern is labelled with letters (aabb, abab, etc.).

Example: Inversnaid is rhymed aabb ccdd — the 2025 fill-in question literally asked for this.

Effect: creates musicality and unity; a regular scheme can feel calm/ordered; broken rhyme can feel uneasy.

How examined: the structural 4-mark question (fill-in / matching) asks you to name stanza, quatrain, couplet, rhyme scheme.

Discuss it: “The regular aabb rhyme creates a sing-song, contained feel that mirrors [theme].”

Rhythm & metre

Definition: the pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables (the “beat”). Iambic pentameter = 5 pairs of unstressed–stressed.

Example: Shakespeare's sonnets use iambic pentameter.

Effect: a steady rhythm feels natural and speech-like; a broken rhythm signals emotion or chaos.

How examined: rarely asked by name in FAL; more often folded into “mood” and “how the poem sounds”.

Discuss it: “The flowing rhythm mirrors the stream's movement / the speaker's calm.”

Enjambment

Definition: a sentence running over a line break without punctuation (the thought continues).

Example: “It drops from the gory lash, / As I whirled it o'er and o'er my head” (The slave dealer).

Effect: creates forward motion, urgency, or a conversational flow; can mirror a rushing or unstoppable action.

How examined: asked when a question focuses on pace/movement; credit for noticing the run-on.

Discuss it: “The enjambment makes the lines run on, mirroring the speaker's unstoppable [emotion/action].”

Caesura

Definition: a strong pause inside a line, usually marked by punctuation ( . , ; : — ).

Example: “Let them be left, O let them be left, wildness and wet.”

Effect: creates a dramatic halt, emphasis, or a reflective, hesitant tone.

How examined: occasionally; credit for linking the pause to a shift in feeling or a key idea.

Discuss it: “The caesura after ‘X' forces a pause that emphasises [word/idea].”

Repetition / Refrain

Definition: repeating a word, phrase or line for emphasis or structure.

Example: “O let them be left, wildness and wet” — the plea is repeated.

Effect: reinforces an idea or emotion; creates rhythm; can sound like a prayer, plea or obsession.

How examined: “Why does the speaker repeat X?” — you explain the emphasis/emotion it creates.

Discuss it: “The repetition of ‘X' underlines the speaker's [emotion], as if he is pleading/insisting.”

Diction & word choice

Definition: the poet's deliberate choice of words and their connotations (emotional associations).

Example: “darksome”, “pitchblack”, “féll-frówning” — ominous, heavy diction in Inversnaid.

Effect: shapes tone and mood; single words carry the poem's feeling.

How examined: “What do these words suggest / reveal?” — you explain what the chosen word implies.

Discuss it: “The word ‘X' has connotations of [idea], which creates a [adjective] tone.”

Meaning & feeling

Tone

Definition: the speaker's attitude/feeling (angry, nostalgic, pleading, ironic…).

Example: Sonnet 73's closing tone is urgent/pleading — “love that well which thou must leave ere long.”

Effect: reveals the speaker's emotion; the exam's tone question is always “what tone + why”.

How examined: “(a) What tone would the speaker use? (b) Why?” — 1 + 1 marks, in every paper.

Discuss it: “The tone is [adjective], because the speaker [reason].”

Mood / Atmosphere

Definition: the feeling the reader gets (tense, peaceful, ominous). Tone = speaker's feeling; mood = reader's feeling.

Example: Inversnaid's pitchblack pool creates an ominous mood.

Effect: pulls the reader into the scene; the “atmosphere” question rewards naming mood + the words that create it.

How examined: “The descriptive words create a [peaceful/tense] atmosphere. Discuss your view.” — 3 marks.

Discuss it: “The mood is [adjective], created by [images/words], because they suggest [idea].”

Theme

Definition: the central idea or message (love, death, nature, guilt, aging).

Example: Sonnet 73 — aging and the urgency of love; Inversnaid — the value of wild nature.

Effect: the poem's “point” — every theme question wants 2–3 supported examples.

How examined: “One of the themes is X. Discuss this theme.” — 3 marks, every paper.

Discuss it: “The theme of [X] is developed through [example 1], [example 2] and [example 3].”

Literal vs figurative meaning

Definition: literal = the surface, dictionary meaning; figurative = the deeper, non-literal meaning.

Example: “That time of year” literally = a season; figuratively = the speaker's old age.

Effect: poems work on two levels; the exam tests that you can see both.

How examined: “Explain the literal meaning” then “the figurative meaning” — 2021 & 2022 asked this explicitly.

Discuss it: “Literally, X means [surface]. Figuratively, it means [deeper idea].”

Speaker vs poet

Definition: the speaker is the voice in the poem (a persona); the poet is the author. They are not the same.

Example: the speaker in “The slave dealer” is the guilt-ridden trader — not Thomas Pringle himself.

Effect: recognising the persona lets you discuss “the speaker's” feelings accurately (the exam always says “the speaker”).

How examined: “What does this reveal about the speaker's character?” — answer about the persona, not the poet.

Discuss it: “The speaker feels [X]…”, never “the poet feels…”.

🎭 Macbeth — Dramatic Techniques & Theory

Macbeth questions test the same literary skills as poetry, plus the specifically dramatic devices. Knowing these lets you answer the “identify + explain” and “director” questions that appear in every paper.

Dramatic irony

Definition: the audience knows something a character does not, making the character's words/actions carry a deeper, often painful meaning.

Example: Duncan praises Macbeth's castle as a “pleasant seat” while the audience knows Macbeth is about to murder him there.

Effect: builds tension and dread; makes the audience feel the tragedy coming.

How examined: “Explain the irony in [X]'s words…” is the most common Macbeth 2-mark question — in every paper.

Discuss it: “It is dramatically ironic that [X] says ‘…', because the audience already knows [truth].”

Soliloquy

Definition: a speech delivered by a character alone on stage, revealing their private thoughts to the audience.

Example: “Is this a dagger which I see before me?” (Act 2 Sc 1); “Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow…” (Act 5 Sc 5).

Effect: gives direct access to a character's inner conflict, ambition, guilt — the audience sees the truth the other characters can't.

How examined: often the extract chosen; questions ask what the soliloquy reveals about the character's state of mind.

Discuss it: “In this soliloquy Macbeth reveals his [emotion] — the audience sees [private thought] that he hides from others.”

Aside

Definition: a short remark a character speaks to the audience (or to themselves) that other characters on stage are not meant to hear.

Example: Macbeth's “(aside) Time, thou anticipatest my dread exploits” (Act 4 Sc 1) — his private plan, hidden from Lennox.

Effect: reveals a character's true intentions while others remain unaware; creates dramatic irony.

How examined: the 2025 paper's extract literally carried the stage direction “(aside)” — questions ask what it reveals about his thoughts.

Discuss it: “Macbeth's aside reveals his [intention] — the audience knows his true plan while [other character] does not.”

Monologue vs soliloquy vs aside

Definition: a monologue is a long speech to others on stage; a soliloquy is spoken alone (private thoughts); an aside is a brief secret remark to the audience.

Example: Macbeth's “Tomorrow…” is a soliloquy; his banquet speeches are monologues; his “(aside)” in Act 4 is an aside.

Effect: each controls how much truth the audience and characters share — the engine of dramatic irony.

How examined: knowing the difference prevents mislabelling in “identify the device” questions.

Discuss it: name the correct form, then say what private truth it exposes.

Symbolism

Definition: objects/events that stand for bigger ideas. Key symbols: blood (guilt), darkness/night (evil), the dagger (murderous intent), sleep (innocence/peace), the witches (chaos/evil), Birnam Wood (nature avenging Duncan).

Example: Lady Macbeth's “Out, damned spot!” — the imaginary blood that symbolises her permanent guilt.

Effect: turns abstract ideas (guilt, ambition) into visible images; unifies the play.

How examined: “Explain what X means/symbolises” — you name the abstract idea behind the image.

Discuss it: “The recurring image of [blood/sleep/darkness] symbolises [guilt/innocence/evil], showing [theme].”

Imagery

Definition: vivid sensory language. Macbeth's dominant images: blood, darkness, clothing (“borrowed robes”), animals, disease/medicine.

Example: “Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood clean from my hand?” — visual, overwhelming blood imagery.

Effect: makes guilt/ambition physically felt; the imagery is the theme made visible.

How examined: theme questions reward citing imagery as evidence (e.g. the blood image as proof of the theme of guilt).

Discuss it: “The image of [X] creates a sense of [mood] and reinforces the theme of [theme].”

Motif

Definition: a repeated image, idea or pattern that recurs through the play (blood, sleep, darkness, unnatural events, “manhood”).

Example: the motif of sleep — Macbeth “murders sleep” and Lady Macbeth sleepwalks.

Effect: repetition builds meaning; tracks a theme's development from start to end.

How examined: theme and “whole play” questions reward tracing a motif across scenes.

Discuss it: “The motif of [X] recurs when [scene 1] and again when [scene 2], charting [development].”

Foreshadowing

Definition: hints or warnings of events to come.

Example: the witches' prophecies; Duncan's remark that “There's no art to find the mind's construction in the face” (foreshadowing his betrayal).

Effect: creates anticipation and dread; makes later events feel inevitable.

How examined: “In stanza 1 the reader is prepared for later events” — the same idea applies to Macbeth scenes; questions ask what a line hints at.

Discuss it: “This foreshadows [later event] — the audience is warned that [outcome] is coming.”

Conflict

Definition: the struggle that drives the plot. External conflict (Macbeth vs Macduff, vs Duncan, vs Malcolm's army); internal conflict (Macbeth's ambition vs his conscience).

Example: Macbeth's dagger soliloquy is pure internal conflict — he wants the crown but recoils from murder.

Effect: creates tension and reveals character; the play's engine.

How examined: “The conflict between Macbeth and Macduff is necessary in the play. Discuss your view.” — a real 3-mark question (2025).

Discuss it: name the type of conflict, the two sides, and what it drives the character to do.

Dramatic tension

Definition: the suspense the audience feels as danger/outcome approaches.

Example: the knocking at the gate after Duncan's murder (Act 2 Sc 2–3) — tension while the Macbeths try to cover their guilt.

Effect: keeps the audience gripped; makes scenes feel urgent.

How examined: folded into “mood/tone” and “director” questions — what action/tone heightens the tension.

Discuss it: “The tension rises when [event], because the audience fears [outcome].”

Stage directions & the “director” lens

Definition: instructions for actors (entrances, exits, actions, sounds like “Alarums”, “Knocking within”).

Example: “A bell rings” before the murder; “Enter the Ghost of Banquo.”

Effect: control pacing, sound and atmosphere — exactly what the director question tests.

How examined: “If you were the director, what would you tell [X] to do? State TWO actions.” — in every paper.

Discuss it: always answer with two physical, stageable actions (gesture, movement, delivery).

Blank verse vs prose

Definition: blank verse = unrhymed iambic pentameter (the nobles' normal speech); prose = ordinary non-metrical speech (often used by the Porter, or to mark madness/chaos).

Example: the Porter's scene is prose; Macbeth's soliloquies are blank verse.

Effect: the shift signals status or a break in order; prose often accompanies comic relief or disorder.

How examined: rarely named in FAL, but useful context for “structure” answers.

Discuss it: note when a character drops from verse to prose — it marks a shift in state or order.

Characterisation

Definition: how Shakespeare builds characters — through their words, actions, what others say, and their development.

Example: Lady Macbeth is built first as ruthless (“unsex me here”), then collapses into guilt-driven madness.

Effect: characters change over the play — the exam rewards tracking that development.

How examined: “What does this reveal about [X]'s character?” — trait + substantiation, in every paper.

Discuss it: “At first [X] is [trait], shown by [evidence], but later becomes [trait], shown by [evidence].”

👑 Macbeth — Complete Revision Guide

Everything examinable, organised for the way the paper actually tests it: characters → themes → key scenes → quotes. The contextual question gives you two extracts, but the 3-mark “whole play” questions demand knowledge of the entire play, so study all of it.

The story in one paragraph (know this cold)

After two Scottish generals, Macbeth and Banquo, defeat the rebel Macdonwald and the invading Norwegians, three witches prophesy that Macbeth will become Thane of Cawdor and then King, and that Banquo's descendants will be kings. When Macbeth is made Thane of Cawdor, the first prophecy comes true and his ambition ignites. Spurred by his wife Lady Macbeth, he murders King Duncan and seizes the throne. Wracked by paranoia, he orders the deaths of Banquo and the family of Macduff. Lady Macbeth descends into guilt-driven madness and dies. Macduff and Duncan's son Malcolm raise an English army, march on Dunsinane hidden by branches from Birnam Wood, and Macduff — “not of woman born” — kills Macbeth. Malcolm is crowned, restoring order.

Characters & their development

Macbeth — the tragic hero

Who he is: a brave, loyal Scottish general who is corrupted by ambition into a tyrant.

Development: (1) honoured warrior praised by Duncan → (2) tempted by the witches' prophecy and his own “black and deep desires” → (3) murders Duncan, then Banquo, then Macduff's family → (4) becomes paranoid and isolated → (5) fights to the end, still clinging to the witches' prophecy, and is killed.

Key traits: ambitious, brave but weak-willed, guilt-ridden, increasingly cruel and paranoid.

Key quotes: “Stars, hide your fires; Let not light see my black and deep desires” (Act 1); “Is this a dagger which I see before me?” (Act 2); “To be thus is nothing, but to be safely thus” (Act 3); “Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow…” (Act 5).

How examined: “Discuss your view” statements like “Macbeth is a tragic hero” or “Macbeth is a victim of the witches” — you argue with 2–3 supported points.

Lady Macbeth — ambition's sharpest edge

Who she is: Macbeth's wife, whose ruthless ambition drives the murder of Duncan.

Development: (1) the stronger, colder partner who plans the murder and steels Macbeth (“screw your courage to the sticking-place”) → (2) covers for him at the banquet when he sees Banquo's ghost → (3) collapses under guilt into sleepwalking, endlessly washing imaginary blood → (4) dies, apparently by suicide.

Key traits: manipulative, ambitious, outwardly strong but inwardly fragile; her fall mirrors Macbeth's.

Key quotes: “Come, you spirits… unsex me here” (Act 1); “A little water clears us of this deed” (Act 2); “Out, damned spot! out, I say!” (Act 5).

How examined: character-reveal questions; the irony of “a little water clears us” vs her later “all the perfumes of Arabia”.

King Duncan — the good king

Who he is: the virtuous, trusting King of Scotland whose murder throws the country into chaos.

Key traits: gracious, generous, just — but fatally trusting (he misreads both the traitor Cawdor and Macbeth).

Key quotes: “There's no art to find the mind's construction in the face” (Act 1 — irony: he says this about the traitor Cawdor, then is betrayed again by Macbeth).

How examined: “Duncan is a good leader. Discuss your view.” (2025) — argue yes (decisive, rewarding) and/or no (too trusting).

Banquo — the honourable foil

Who he is: Macbeth's fellow general; the witches say his descendants, not he, will be kings.

Key traits: noble, cautious, suspicious of the witches (“The instruments of darkness tell us truths… to win us to our harm”).

Development: resists temptation; Macbeth, threatened by the prophecy about Banquo's line, has him murdered — but Banquo's son Fleance escapes.

How examined: character-reveal and irony questions; his ghost returning at the banquet is a favourite extract.

Macduff — the avenger

Who he is: the loyal Thane of Fife who discovers Duncan's body and ultimately kills Macbeth.

Key traits: loyal, honest, grief-stricken but just; the one “not of woman born” (born by caesarean).

Development: flees to England to raise Malcolm's army; when Macbeth slaughters his wife and children, his grief (“All my pretty ones?”) steels his revenge.

Key quotes: “He has no children” (Act 4); “Turn, hell-hound, turn!” (Act 5).

How examined: “The conflict between Macbeth and Macduff is necessary. Discuss.” (2025) — his revenge is the play's resolution.

Malcolm — the rightful king

Who he is: Duncan's elder son and heir.

Key traits: cautious (flees to England), wise (tests Macduff's loyalty with a false confession of vices), a healer-figure who restores order.

Development: from frightened prince to the leader who retakes Scotland and becomes king.

How examined: the “order restored” theme; his testing of Macduff shows wisdom vs Macbeth's rashness.

The Witches (Weird Sisters)

Who they are: supernatural agents of chaos who tempt Macbeth with half-truths.

Key traits: ambiguous, manipulative; they “lie like truth” — technically true prophecies that mislead.

Key quotes: “Fair is foul, and foul is fair” (Act 1 — the play's moral inversion); “none of woman born shall harm Macbeth” (Act 4).

How examined: “The witches are to blame for the chaos in Scotland. Discuss your view.” (2025) — argue blame vs Macbeth's own choice.

Minor characters (the matching question loves these)

Ross: the messenger/nobleman who reports events (battle, the “unnatural” horses).

Fleance: Banquo's son, who escapes — his survival means Banquo's line may yet be kings.

Young Siward: English general's son, killed by Macbeth — his father accepts the honourable death.

The Doctor: attends Lady Macbeth; “Were I from Dunsinane away and clear, profit again should hardly draw me here” — a witness to the corruption.

The Porter: comic relief (drunken gatekeeper) that follows the murder with dark humour.

How examined: the 4-mark Column A→B matching question (3.1.1) tests exactly these identifications every year.

Major themes & how they develop

Ambition

The idea: unchecked ambition corrupts and destroys. Macbeth's ambition is awakened by the prophecy and Lady Macbeth; it “o'erleaps itself” into murder, then more murder to hold power, until it destroys him.

Evidence: Macbeth's “vaulting ambition”; Lady Macbeth's “unsex me”; Banquo's resistance as the contrast.

How examined: “One of the themes is ambition. Discuss this theme.” — cite the prophecy, the murder, and the tyrant's paranoia.

Guilt & conscience

The idea: evil deeds cannot be washed clean. Guilt appears as blood and sleeplessness.

Evidence: “Macbeth doth murder sleep”; “Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood clean?”; Lady Macbeth's “Out, damned spot!” sleepwalking.

How examined: symbolism/irony questions (the blood image; “a little water clears us of this deed” vs her madness).

Appearance vs reality / deception

The idea: “Fair is foul, and foul is fair” — things are not what they seem.

Evidence: the Macbeths' welcoming faces vs murderous intent; Duncan's inability to “find the mind's construction in the face”; the witches' half-truths.

How examined: irony questions are essentially this theme — always link “what seems” vs “what is”.

Order vs chaos / the natural order

The idea: the King is divinely appointed; killing him plunges nature itself into chaos.

Evidence: unnatural events after Duncan's murder — horses eating each other, darkness at noon, an owl killing a falcon (Act 2 Sc 4).

How examined: “why is the following statement FALSE / theme of order” — the chaos is the proof of the crime against nature.

Manhood / masculinity

The idea: what it means to be a man. Lady Macbeth equates manhood with violence (“When you durst do it, then you were a man”); Macduff redefines it as the courage to feel grief (“I must also feel it as a man”).

Evidence: the “manhood” challenge (Act 1 Sc 7); Macduff's grief (Act 4 Sc 3).

How examined: “One of the themes is manhood” — 2024 asked this explicitly.

Loyalty & kingship

The idea: true loyalty (Banquo, Macduff, Malcolm) contrasts with treachery (Cawdor, Macbeth). A good king (Duncan, Malcolm) is just and orderly; a tyrant (Macbeth) rules by fear.

Evidence: Macduff's loyalty to Scotland over Macbeth; Malcolm's testing of Macduff; the play ends with order restored under Malcolm.

How examined: “Duncan is a good leader” / “good ultimately triumphs over evil” (2022) — argument questions.

Fate vs free will

The idea: do the witches cause Macbeth's fall, or does he choose it? The play leans to choice: Macbeth hears the prophecy but decides to act on it, and the witches' “none of woman born” is a trap he walks into.

Evidence: Banquo hears the same prophecies but resists; Macbeth “wouldst not play false, and yet wouldst wrongly win.”

How examined: “The witches are to blame for the chaos. Discuss your view.” (2025) — the classic version of this theme.

How to answer Macbeth contextual questions (quick method)

  1. Read the extract and the scene label under it — it tells you where you are in the play.
  2. Anchor every answer to the line number the question gives you — quote or point to it.
  3. For 2-mark questions: point + reason, in one sentence.
  4. For “Refer to the drama as a whole”: step outside the extract and use events from elsewhere in the play — these 3-mark questions are impossible to score on the extract alone.
  5. For “Discuss your view”: take a side, then give 2–3 supported points (a mix is even stronger).
  6. Director questions: two physical actions, never feelings.

⏱ Examination Strategy — Max Marks in 2½ Hours

The paper's own instructions tell you to spend about 75 minutes per section. Use every mark as a clock.

1 · Time allocation

BlockTimeWhat you do
First 5 min0–5Read instructions; tick your two sections on the checklist (Drama + Poetry only).
Macbeth~75 minRead extract 3.1 → answer; read extract 3.2 → answer (≈35 marks).
Poetry~70 minRead poem 6.1 → answer; read poem 6.2 → answer (≈35 marks).
Final 5 min—Check numbering, spelling-that-changes-meaning, and that you answered BOTH parts of each 3-mark question.
⌛ The 2-minutes-per-mark rule 70 marks in 150 minutes ≈ 2 minutes per mark. A 3-mark “discuss” question deserves ~6 minutes; a 1-mark multiple choice ~30 seconds. Don't spend 10 minutes polishing a 1-mark answer.

2 · Interpret the question before writing

  1. Circle the command word (explain/discuss/identify/describe/substantiate) — it fixes the answer's shape.
  2. Circle the marks — the mark value tells you how many points to give.
  3. Note the line reference — “Refer to line 5” means your answer must use line 5, not the whole poem.
  4. Note “as a whole” — this flips it: step outside the extract and use the whole text.
  5. Note “State TWO” — exactly two, no more, no less.

3 · Produce memo-shaped answers

  • Answer in the order asked and number exactly as the paper does (3.1.2, 6.2.4…).
  • One fact = one mark. A 2-mark answer has two clear pieces; a 3-mark answer has 2–3 supported points.
  • Lead with the mark-earning word (the tone word, the device, the trait), then attach the reason.
  • Use the memo's connectives: “because”, “in the same way that… so too”, “yet/but” (for irony), “this shows that…”.
  • Quote only when asked, and don't waste time on inverted commas — you're not penalised for omitting them.

4 · Avoid the mark-losers

  • Hedging with two answers — the first is marked. Commit to one.
  • Opinion without substantiation — “I think he's good” earns nothing on a 3-mark question.
  • Staying inside the extract on “as a whole” questions.
  • Answering a novel/short-story question — you answer Drama + Poetry only.
  • Wrong device name — learn metaphor vs simile vs personification vs alliteration vs assonance precisely.
  • Padding — long answers don't earn extra; they cost you the next question.

5 · A full-mark answer checklist

  • ☑ Command word respected (name / explain / discuss / substantiate).
  • ☑ Right number of points for the mark value.
  • ☑ Grounded in the text (quote or line reference).
  • ☑ “Discuss” answers take a clear stance and give 2–3 reasons.
  • ☑ Spelling that could change meaning is corrected.
  • ☑ Numbering matches the question paper.
